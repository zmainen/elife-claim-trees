Design note · 2026-09-11

The reader

The site has no view of the paper. It has views of what we made from the paper, and it asks every reader to start there. This note proposes that the paper page begin with the paper, and that claims be reached from it — from the sentence that makes one and the figure panel that shows one — with the structure one click further and the graph two.

Who is reading

The audience the page was never designed for.

The page as it stands was built by the people who built the graph, for themselves. Its vocabulary is the graph's vocabulary: H1.P2.1, dissociates-with, scope qualifiers, eliminations & validating controls. That vocabulary is exact, and it is the reason the corpus is auditable. It is also a second language, and the reader we are now asked to serve — an editor, a reviewer, a scientist from the next field over, someone at eLife deciding whether any of this is worth their time — does not speak it and should not have to.

What that reader does know is how to read a paper. They have done it thousands of times. They know where the abstract is, that the figures carry the results, that a sentence with a p-value in it is a claim. The one familiar object in this whole enterprise is the paper, and it is the one thing the site does not show.

The paper is the interface. A claim is reached from the place in the paper where it is made. Structure is what you see when you ask for it, never what you have to get past.

The paper itself—Absent. The one element with no learning curve is the one that is missing.

None of these elements is wrong. Each is the honest surface of something real in the corpus, and the previous note was right to sort them by category. The trouble is altitude: the page opens at the level of the graph and never descends to the level of the paper. A reader who cannot find the paper cannot tell whether the graph is about anything.

Three depths

One page, three ways of standing back from it. Each answers a question the previous one raises.

DepthAnswersBuilt from
Paper defaultWhat does this paper say, and where does it say it?The JATS article (prepare already fetches it), the figures (the IIIF URLs every claim already carries), and the sentence marks the coverage layer writes to marked/.
FindingsWhat did they find, figure by figure, and did it hold up?Claims with a panel:, ordered by figure and panel; the verification records, restated in one sentence.
ArgumentHow does it fit together? What did they rule out? What does every result assume?Hypotheses → entails → predictions → tested by → results; alternatives with ruled out by; scope claims. The same graph, read down one path.

The claim card is the same at every depth. Open a claim from an underlined sentence, from a figure panel, from a Findings row, or from a hypothesis chain, and the same card opens in the same rail. There is one drawer, as the previous note asked, and it belongs to the reader rather than to a view.

The paper view

A clean article, with one accent that means “a claim lives here”.

Choices, and why

Two typefaces, and the split carries meaning. The paper's own words are set in a serif; everything we add — labels, the rail, the card, the plain wordings — is set in a sans. A reader never has to ask whether a sentence is the authors' or ours. The current site sets everything in Inter, and the generated summary reads as if it were the abstract.

One accent. A single teal means “a claim is here”: the underline under a sentence, the heading under a figure, the rail's label. Green, amber and red are reserved for the re-run outcome and appear only as a dot or a verdict chip, so colour on the page always answers exactly one question: did it hold up? The previous note's palette rule — green means correct, never our effort — is kept.

The figure is the unit of findings. Under each figure, a short list: the panel letters, the plain wording of the claim from that panel, the re-run dot. This is where the design does its main work. It is the panel-level claim the README argues for, shown at the panel, in the paper, in the order the authors chose. Figure 1, which sets up the task, says so and lists nothing.

Statistics stay in the paper. The article text keeps every t, β and confidence interval, because that is the paper. The claims layer never repeats them in a row; the card shows the authors' wording under the plain one, and the comparison of the paper's value against our re-run, in a monospace pair, where a reader who wants the number can find it.

Methods fold; Introduction and Discussion do not. The reader who came for the argument reads the Introduction and the Discussion; the Methods are there for the one who came to check something, one click down.

The claim card

One card, in the order a reader asks.

1 · Kind, then the claim in plain words

FINDING · RE-RUN MATCHES
The left anterior insula responds more when a partner loses because of the participant's choice.

Anterior insula BOLD activity is significantly elevated in the Social condition compared to the Partner condition specifically after negative partner outcomes…

The kinds are eight plain nouns: Finding, Check, Hypothesis, Prediction, Interpretation, Caveat, From the literature, Alternative ruled out. They map one-to-one onto role × stance, so nothing is lost; only the names change.

2 · Where

Figure 4 E, F · In the text. Each is a link back into the paper. The card is never a dead end.

3 · Does it hold up?

A verdict chip and one sentence: We re-ran this from the authors' deposited data and got the same result. Then Paper says / Our re-run as a pair. For a hypothesis or a prediction the block is absent, because there is nothing to re-run and the current drawer's Not yet assessed banner implied otherwise.

4 · How it was measured

The method: field, verbatim. One line.

5 · How it connects

The relations, translated and de-duplicated. Each linked claim appears once, under the strongest label that applies, and every one is a button to the next card, with a back stack. The full vocabulary survives at the bottom as Full record ↗.

Findings and Argument

The two deeper views, and what they keep from today's page.

Findings is the paper view with the prose removed: each figure as a thumbnail, its findings beside it, one plain line each with the panel and the dot. It is the digest a reader wants who has decided not to read the paper, and it is the same rows the paper view shows under each figure, so the two never disagree.

Argument is today's Logic of the claims, read down one path instead of seven. Each hypothesis becomes a question; under it, what it predicts; under each prediction, the results that tested it, with their dots. Then the alternatives the authors ruled out, struck through, each with the result that ruled it out. Then the caveats every result inherits — for Gädeke, that the partner was an algorithm, and that there were two studies. The seven collapsed sections, the dissociation pairs and the H1.P2.1 addresses are gone from the surface; the edges that produced them are what the view is drawn from, and the graph is one link away for the reader who wants all of it.

Kept, and whyThe Hypotheses · Claims · Inferences summary was the best thing on the old page and stays first, relabelled They asked · They found · It means. The counts line stays, in one sentence in the rail. The generated three paragraphs and the plain claim wordings are the two places a model writes prose the reader sees; both are labelled as such.

What it costs

Three things the corpus does not have yet, and one it has for one paper.

NeedState todayWork
A plain wording per claimshortClaim is filled for hypotheses and predictions and null for every empirical claim, so every row on the site opens with statistics. The prototype's 33 wordings were written by hand.A plain-claim layer: one model call per paper, one sentence per claim, no numbers, the authors' terms kept. It is a layer, so it gets a version, a ledger entry and a place for a person to approve it — which this prose, of all the prose on the site, should have.
The article's section treeprepare fetches the JATS and keeps flattened text: results_text is one string, headings glued to sentences. The prototype re-parsed the XML.Keep the fetched XML beside prepared.json (it is cached already), or have prepare write the section tree. Either way the site renders the article from JATS, which is what eLife publishes and what the design is named for.
Sentence marks for every papermarked/ holds one file, Gädeke. The coverage layer that writes it has run for one paper.Run coverage for the other nine. The marks are what turns an underline on; without them the paper view is still a clean paper, only the sentences are silent.
Marks on captions and tablesOf Gädeke's 64 sentence marks, 31 fall in the results text and the rest in captions and the table. The prototype shows the caption marks under the figure via panel:, not as underlines.Nothing for now. The figure list is the better surface for a caption mark; a table view can come when a paper needs one.

One thing the prototype leaves out on purpose. Fourteen of Gädeke's marked results carry no claim — the gap marks the coverage adjudicator records. They are the most honest thing in the corpus and the hardest to show without clutter. The proposal is a single quiet line in the rail, 14 results in the text have no claim yet, linking to the coverage layer, rather than a second kind of underline in the paper.

Order of work

  1. Declare the plain-claim layer and run it for the ten papers. Smallest change, largest effect: every existing view gets a readable first line the same day, before any page changes.
  2. Keep the JATS and add a renderer — sections, paragraphs, inline markup, figure references — as a component. The prototype's build_reader.py is the spec: 74 paragraphs, 5 figures, 41 marks resolved for Gädeke.
  3. Run coverage for the other nine papers so every article has marks.
  4. Make the reader the paper page. /papers/<paper>/ opens on the Paper view; ?view=findings and ?view=argument are the two depths; today's structure view becomes the Argument view's source. The claim drawer is rewritten as the card and keeps its open-claim event so nothing that opens it breaks.
  5. Demote Artifacts and Pipeline to a second, quieter row under the title — About this record — with the graph reachable from the Argument view. They lose nothing but their place beside the paper.

Steps 1–3 are pipeline work and change no page. Step 4 is the design. Step 5 is the smallest and should wait until the reader is in front of someone who is not us.
