Every other subcommand asks whether a claim is right. These two ask whether the claim tree is *complete* — what does the paper assert that nothing in the tree accounts for. It is a different question, it needs a different denominator, and neither `evaluate` nor any agreement score can answer it.

Both commands are free. They make no model calls, read the paper from cache after the first fetch, and finish in seconds. That is deliberate: an audit you can afford to run on every paper, every time, is worth more than a better audit you run on the papers you already suspect.

## Why agreement scores cannot see this

`evaluate` scores a fresh extraction against a curated reference: claim recovery, panel agreement, role agreement. It is the right tool for "did this prompt change make things worse", and it is blind to the failure that matters most here.

Two claim sets can agree perfectly about a third of a paper. Neither of them mentions the other two thirds; the score is 100%; nothing in the output hints that anything is missing. The denominator is the reference claim set, so whatever the reference missed is invisible by construction.

`coverage` takes its denominator from the paper. It inventories the panels and the statistics, segments the entire text into sentences, and asks of each sentence: does it carry a result, and if so, does any claim state that result. What both claim sets missed then shows up as a sentence with nothing pointing at it.

## What coverage measures

Three inventories, reported separately because they fail differently.

**Panels.** Every figure panel, supplementary figure and appendix table the paper defines, against the panels claims cite. Gädeke: 20 of 40 accounted for. It also reports panels *cited at figure level rather than panel level* — a claim that says "Figure 3" when the paper's Figure 3 has six panels has not really anchored itself, and this is the only place that shows up.

**Statistics.** Every reported test statistic, p-value and confidence interval, against those claims restate. Gädeke: 46 of 90.

**Spans.** Every sentence in the abstract, results, captions and tables, classified as carrying a result or not, and if it does, matched against the claim set. Methods sentences are excluded unless you pass `--include-methods`, because the corpus claims results rather than procedure. Gädeke: 245 spans, of which 93 carry a statistic or name a panel and 152 are prose that asserts no result.

The span inventory is the one that matters, and its raw output overstates the problem by roughly a factor of two.

## The mechanical match is a proxy, and wrong in both directions

The match asks: does some claim restate this statistic, or name this panel. That is a proxy for the question you care about — does some claim account for what this sentence asserts — and it fails in both directions.

It **misses coverage** when a claim states an effect without repeating its numbers. The claim is doing its job; the string comparison cannot see it.

It **invents obligations** when a span carries a statistic but asserts nothing of its own: a cross-reference ("detailed results are reported in Appendix 1—table 1"), analysis narration ("we then fitted the model described in Equation 3"), a bare panel label in a caption.

For Gädeke the unadjudicated match leaves 36 spans unaccounted for. Twenty-two of those are the matcher being wrong. Reporting all 36 as unmet obligations buries the fourteen that are real — which is precisely the distinction the exercise exists to draw.

## Adjudication: judged once, stored, auditable

So the residue is judged by hand and the judgement is written down, in `mappings/<slug>.json`. Three verdicts:

| Verdict | Meaning |
|:--------|:--------|
| `covered` | A claim does account for it; the matcher could not see that. Names the claim. |
| `gap` | Nothing in the tree accounts for it. A real hole. |
| `not-an-assertion` | The span states no result of its own — cross-reference, narration, bare label. |

```json
{
  "paper": "gadeke-2026-guilt-insula",
  "adjudicated": "2026-09-09",
  "spans": [
    {
      "uid": "results-016",
      "verdict": "gap",
      "claim": null,
      "why": "Figure 2C/F caption asserts a real finding — higher fitted risk-aversion parameter in Social than Solo in Study 1 only — and no claim covers the rho parameter.",
      "sha": "612e5993"
    }
  ]
}
```

A bare array of those objects is accepted too, since that is a natural thing for an adjudicator to hand back.

The verdicts are **stored rather than recomputed**. A judgement that is recomputed each run is a judgement nobody can disagree with, because there is nothing to point at; one that is stored can be audited, argued with, and reused when the corpus changes around it.

<Aside type="caution" title="Verdicts fail closed">
Each verdict carries `sha`, the fingerprint of the sentence it was made about. Span ids are positional — `results-026` means the 26th span of the Results section — so inserting one sentence earlier in the section shifts every id after it, and a verdict would silently reattach to different text.

When the fingerprint does not match, the verdict is **discarded** and the span goes back to being unexamined. The command warns and carries on. That is recoverable; applying the verdict anyway would be a wrong judgement wearing the authority of a human decision.
</Aside>

## Running it

```bash
elife-extract coverage \
  --doi 10.7554/eLife.105391 \
  --paper-slug gadeke-2026-guilt-insula \
  --claims-dir ./claims/gadeke-2026-guilt-insula \
  --mapping ./mappings/gadeke-2026-guilt-insula.json \
  --json ./out/coverage-gadeke.json
```

Note `--claims-dir` points at **one paper's directory**, not at the corpus root — `coverage` does not take `--corpus-dir` and does not need the rest of the corpus.

The result for Gädeke: 245 spans, 78 carrying a result, 64 accounted for, 15 asserting nothing, **14 real gaps, nothing unexamined**. The denominator falls from 93 to 78 because the fifteen spans that assert nothing were never obligations in the first place.

`--json` writes the whole report — panel inventory, statistic inventory, every orphan span with its section, text, statistics and panels — for tooling that wants the numbers rather than the page.

### As a gate

```bash
elife-extract coverage --fail-on-orphans …
```

Exits non-zero if any panel or result is unaccounted for. Free and fast enough to run over the whole corpus in CI. Note that on this corpus it will fail: fourteen gaps are fourteen gaps, and the honest thing for a gate to do is say so.

## `mark` — the answer, written into the paper

A count in a report is something you audit. A document with the assignments in it is something you read.

```bash
elife-extract mark \
  --doi 10.7554/eLife.105391 \
  --paper-slug gadeke-2026-guilt-insula \
  --claims-dir ./claims/gadeke-2026-guilt-insula \
  --mapping ./mappings/gadeke-2026-guilt-insula.json \
  -o ./marked/gadeke-2026-guilt-insula.marked.md
```

Every span carrying a result gets a tika v2 note saying what became of it:

```
⟦>zach claim=<claim-uuid>: @{the quoted sentence} what the claim says⟧
⟦>zach claim=no-assertion: @{the quoted sentence} why it asserts nothing⟧
⟦>zach claim=gap: @{the quoted sentence} why no claim covers it⟧
```

A sentence with **no** mark carries no result and was never an obligation. That is the only thing an unmarked sentence means.

### Why the gap is marked rather than left bare

The first version recorded a gap by absence. It reads well as a principle and fails on the page: 152 of this paper's 245 spans are ordinary prose, so absence is dominated by sentences that were never obligations, and a bare line could mean either "no claim accounts for this result" or "there is no result here" — the one distinction the whole exercise exists to draw. Rendering it is what showed this; the argument for absence had sounded fine.

### Why the payload is a UUID

The mark carries the claim's UUID, not its slug. A slug is derived from the claim's text, so rewording a claim changes it and a mark carrying one rots silently. Every claim in the corpus has a UUID, and `export_mira.py` uses the same UUID as its node identity — so the mark, the claim file and the MIRA node all name the same thing.

The note's body carries the claim's short text as well, because a margin card showing only `49d08c4f-7b31-4967-9ab7-06aa50a006b4` is correct, durable and unreadable. The key stays the identity; the body says what it means.

### Why the anchor is a quote

A tika note anchors to a quoted span and relocates that quote when the text moves. An assignment written this way survives edits that neither a position nor a hash can: reorder the Results section and the marks follow their sentences; fix a typo and the note is still attached to the same claim about the same result.

The quote is the span **verbatim, never truncated**. An earlier version cut anchors at 120 characters with an ellipsis, producing quotes that appear nowhere in the paper — tika could then not locate 78 of 93 anchors, those notes collapsed to zero-width points, and the spans they were about were never highlighted. It looked like a missing editor feature and was a malformed document.

### The round-trip check

```
=== Marked — gadeke-2026-guilt-insula ===
  assigned to a claim : 64
  states no result    : 15
  unclaimed (gap)     : 14
  unmarked prose      : (carries no result — never an obligation)
  written             : ./marked/gadeke-2026-guilt-insula.marked.md
  round-trip          : OK — 93 marks read back identically
```

The last line is the check that matters. `mark` parses the marks straight back out of the document it just wrote and compares them to the assignments they came from. If the document cannot reproduce them, it is not a record of anything, and the command exits non-zero.

## Where this sits in the workflow

```
extract → write → verify-refs        induce the tree
    ↓
coverage                             what did it miss? (mechanical)
    ↓
adjudicate → mappings/<slug>.json    judge the residue, once
    ↓
coverage --mapping                   the real gap count
    ↓
mark                                 the answer, in the document
```
