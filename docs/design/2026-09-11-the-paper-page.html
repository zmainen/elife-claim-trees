The paper page

The page has four tabs and they are four different kinds of thing. One is the paper's content, two are the output of layers, and one is a call to an external service. The tab bar is a category error, and every other complaint about the page follows from it.

Subject /papers/<paper> Follows layers-as-pipeline Status implemented

What the page is for

Before deciding what belongs on it, what is it asked?

A reader arriving at a paper page wants, in this order, four things. What does this paper claim? How do those claims hold each other up? What can I take away and use? And only then, how was this made, and how far has it got?

That order is not arbitrary. The first is the paper; the last is us. A reader who cannot get the first is not helped by the fourth, and a page that leads with provenance is a page about its own machinery. Everything on the page should be one of those four, and should appear roughly in that order.

A view is a way of looking at this paper. If a tab is a way of looking at something else — at a layer, at the corpus, at a service — it is not a view, and it does not belong in the same row as the ones that are.

The category error

Set the four tabs beside each other and ask what each is a view of.

TabA view of…Category
Claimsthis paper's claimsthe paper
Abstract ↔ claimsthe abstract-map layer's outputa layer
Argument from graphan undeclared synthesis outputa layer, unaccounted
Reviewa request to a review servicean external service

Four tabs, three categories, and the row offers no clue which is which. That is why the set “makes no sense” on sight: the reader is asked to choose between things that are not alternatives to one another. Choosing Claims instead of Review is not like choosing between two ways of reading a paper; it is like choosing between a chapter and a printing press.

The duplication follows from the same error. abstract-map now appears twice on one page — once as a node in the layer map, once as a tab — because the page never decided whether a layer's output is a view of the paper or a thing the pipeline owns. It is the second. The pipeline already has a surface for it, and that surface is the one this note's predecessor built.

Two drawers, one edge

Why the map and the content are fighting.

The layer map sits above the tabs, so its drawer and the claim drawer are reachable at the same moment and slide from the same edge. Opening a layer covers the claims you were about to read; opening a claim covers the layer you were checking. Neither knows about the other, so both can be open, and the page has two panels stacked over its own content.

The map also takes 360 px of the page's most valuable space to say something the reader has not yet asked. The content it displaces is the answer to question one.

Four views, one category

Every tab becomes a way of looking at this paper, and they run in the order the reader asks for them.

ViewAnswersBuilt from
Claims default What does this paper claim? Today's ClaimStructure, unchanged.
Graph How do the claims hold each other up? ClaimDAG.tsx, which already exists and is imported nowhere. 14 relation types with real edges — Gädeke alone carries 29 scopes, 13 validates, 6 rules-out.
Artifacts What can I take away? Every file this paper produced, from the cells' produces: MIRA, the extended encoding, OXA, Discourse Graphs, the gap report, coverage, the marked paper. The resolver already exists.
Pipeline How was this made, how far has it got? The layer map, moved down from above the tabs. It gets the full panel height, and its drawer stops competing.

Note what this buys beyond tidiness. Artifacts is the view the corpus has never had: the exports have been published at /exports/ for weeks with nothing on the site linking them. Graph is the view the project is named for. Both were one decision away, and the decision was blocked by a tab bar with no room in it for a fourth thing of a different kind.

What moves into the pipeline

Three tabs leave. They do not disappear; two become what they always were.

Leaves asArrives asWork
Abstract ↔ claims abstract-map — already a declared layer, already in the map, already has a cell. Its view moves to /papers/<paper>/abstract-map and into the layer drawer. Move a component. No schema change.
Argument from graph A new synthesis layer, needs: [claim-tree, abstract-map], produces: site/src/data/synthesis-v3/{paper}.json. Declare it. The artifact exists for 12 papers and no layer accounts for it — the pipeline's own accounting is short by one.
Review Not a layer. layers.yaml already says so in as many words: review is an operation on a version, not a step. The persona service is a different product and should be reached from somewhere that is not a paper page. Remove the tab. Decide separately where the service lives.

The test for whether something is a tab: could it be a layer? If yes, it is a layer, and the pipeline already knows how to show it — with a version, a ledger entry, a staleness state and an approval it has not got. A tab has none of those, which is exactly why synthesis has been drifting unversioned in a tab for months.

The header, demoted

One smaller thing, because it is on the way. The green L4 · We re-analysed their deposited data banner is the most prominent element on a page about someone else's work, and what it reports is our own effort. The vocabulary was fixed; the prominence was not. It becomes a line among the counts.

Order of work

  1. Move the layer map into a Pipeline tab. Smallest change, biggest relief: it ends the two-drawer collision and gives the content back its space. Nothing is removed.
  2. Add the Artifacts view. Pure gain — the resolver and the published files already exist, and nothing on the site links them today.
  3. Add the Graph view from the component already written. Check it in dark mode first: it hard-codes #ffffff, which is the bug the palette tokens were introduced to end.
  4. Declare the synthesis layer and move Argument from graph to it.
  5. Move Abstract ↔ claims to abstract-map.
  6. Decide where the persona service lives, and remove the Review tab.

Steps 1–3 are additive and safe to do before a demo. Steps 4–6 remove things and are worth taking slowly, because each one is a claim about what the pipeline is for.

All six are done. Step 4 turned out to be the one that paid: declaring synthesis revealed that seven of the ten published papers had a synthesis nobody could reach, because the tab read a three-file directory and the v3 run had written twelve to another. An output no layer declares is not merely unversioned — it is unfindable, and nobody notices, which is the argument for the pipeline in one example.
