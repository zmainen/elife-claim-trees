# Warrant: a claim's argument, read from the tree

**Status:** proposed
**Issue:** [#126](https://github.com/zmainen/elife-claim-trees/issues/126)
**Frames:** [#20](https://github.com/zmainen/elife-claim-trees/issues/20) · [#28](https://github.com/zmainen/elife-claim-trees/issues/28) · [#36](https://github.com/zmainen/elife-claim-trees/issues/36) · [#31](https://github.com/zmainen/elife-claim-trees/issues/31)
**Depends on:** `docs/claim-format.md`, the `prediction-outcome` and `stance` layers, `scripts/relations.py`, `scripts/export_mira.py`

The ruling on #20 kept an epistemic assessment on every claim and rejected both ways it has been
filled: not the analyst's hand-written strength, which nobody has written, and not the paper's
own confidence, which is already on the assertion. It should be derived from the evaluation of
the actual evidence, it needs an operational definition, and it needs a better name. A version-1
draft proposed the name, a rule, the layer that computes it, and what happens to the field it
replaces. The maintainer ruled on that draft the same day (2026-09-13), and the rulings reframe
the layer. This note is version 2.

## What the format already says, and what it does not do

`docs/claim-format.md` states the principle in its section on conditionality: the epistemic
status of a claim is bounded by the weakest claim it requires, "not by annotation, but by graph
traversal", and a reproduction that fails moves a claim to `contested`. Nothing computes the
first. The field is typed by hand at extraction, which is why it carries the role name on 77
claims, an undefined value on 44, and `contested` on none.

Three things the field has been asked to hold now live elsewhere. The paper's confidence in a
result is `confidence` on the assertion, set by the readers from the paper's verbs. Whether the
paper asserts a claim or raises it to reject is `stance`. Whether a number reproduced is the
reproduction record, which a checking layer reads. What is left is the thing the ruling asks for:
given the argument the paper makes, how well is the claim supported.

## The name

`warrant`. It is what the tree's argument gives a reader grounds to believe, as distinct from
what the paper says (`confidence`) and from how many readers agreed (`READER_CONFIDENCE` in the
contract). `vocabulary.py` already uses the word for the examples of claims that warrant others.

## The reframe

Version 1 mixed two things the maintainer's rulings separate. It read the paper's confidence into
the floor, and it read reproduction records — a `verified` number lifted a claim to `strong`, a
`mismatch` dropped it to `contested`. Both are wrong for warrant, because both are *checking*,
and checking is not what warrant is.

Warrant reasons from what the paper reports and how its argument hangs together, as the tree
records it: a hypothesis commits to predictions; results test those predictions and come out as
predicted or not; controls validate results and rule out rivals; claims support, require and
interpret one another. That structure is the paper's case for its conclusions, and warrant reads
how well the case holds together — nothing more. A confident sentence earns no warrant its
argument does not; a hedged one loses none it does.

## The inputs, as they stand

Everything the rule needs is the argument structure in the claim files today.

| Input | Where | Coverage now |
|:--|:--|:--|
| Prediction outcomes | `tests` with `confirms` or `refutes` on a prediction (#28) | 39 predictions tested; outcomes on 34 after the #28 re-run of Gädeke, more as papers re-run |
| Controls | incoming `validates` | 34 claims |
| Rivals | `rules-out` to a claim with stance `rejects`; `addresses` for same-question matching | 12 alternatives |
| Confirmations | outgoing `confirms` — a result that tested a prediction and it came out as predicted | one per tested prediction |
| Support | incoming `supports`, `extends`, `confirms`, `refutes` | across the tree |
| Dependencies | outgoing `requires` and `part-of`, with the required claim's role | 0 `requires` to scope or assumption claims today, which is itself a finding for the readers |

Reproduction records, verification provenance and the paper's confidence are deliberately *not*
inputs to warrant. They are inputs to the checking layers below.

## The rule, version 2

The rule is deliberately coarse, because a fine rule with no adjudicated readings behind it is a
guess with more digits. Four graded levels, one of them a flag — `strong`, `moderate`, `weak`,
`contested` — plus the prediction and alternative vocabularies. The rule is by role, and every
value it writes carries the inputs that fired. It reads the argument alone.

**A prediction** has an outcome, not a warrant: `confirmed`, `refuted`, `untested`, from #28. The
field is still written, so a reader sees one word per claim, and the site shows it as an outcome.

**An alternative** (a hypothesis with stance `rejects` or `entertains`) is `ruled-out` when a
`rules-out` edge reaches it, else `open`. Naming the ruling-out claim says what the paper did.

**A hypothesis** is warranted by its predictions and its rivals:

- `contested` if any of its predictions is `refuted`;
- `strong` if at least one prediction is `confirmed`, none refuted, and every rival is ruled out;
- `moderate` if at least one prediction is `confirmed` but a rival still stands;
- `weak` if no prediction has an outcome.

**An empirical, control, methodological or scope claim** is read from the argument alone:

- `strong` if the claim both tests a prediction that came out as predicted (it is the source of a
  `confirms`) and is validated by a control;
- `moderate` if exactly one of those holds, or if it is supported by two or more distinct claims;
- `contested` if it is the target of a `refutes` from another claim in the tree;
- `weak` otherwise. A claim with an empty dossier is `weak` and flagged `unassessed` in the fired
  keys — the argument records nothing that bears on it, which is a finding, not a default.

There is no confidence clause and no reproduction clause. Both belong to a later layer.

**An interpretation** sits one step below the strongest claim it interprets, capped at `moderate`
— argument alone never lifts an interpretation to `strong` — and `weak` if it interprets nothing
graded.

**A synthesis** reads agreement among what it interprets: `moderate` if it draws two or more
claims together and none of them is `weak`; `weak` otherwise; capped at `moderate`.

**Propagation**, from the format's own rule: a claim's warrant is bounded above by the weakest
claim it `requires` — but of the same role group only. Empirical and control bound each other;
hypothesis and prediction bound each other; methodological and scope never bound anything, and
are never bounded. A required claim of another kind that is unassessed is listed in the fired
keys as `prerequisite-unassessed:<slug>` and changes no level, so the gap is surfaced without
being silently counted. With zero `requires` edges to scope or assumption claims today this
mostly changes nothing, and it starts working the first time a reader writes one.

## Checks are separate layers

Verification (reproductions), a methods assessment, a statistics check and a citation check are
each a *later* layer that writes a modifier beside the warrant, explicitly. Warrant states what
the paper's argument implies; a checking layer states whether that argument survives scrutiny,
and it may modify what warrant said, on the record.

`contested` from a failed reproduction is the verification layer's word, not warrant's. Warrant
reaches `contested` only when the tree's own argument turns on the claim — a result the paper
itself reports as refuting it. A reproduction that does not match is checking, and it is the
verification layer that declares the modifier when it runs.

The first such layer to declare is verification, because its records already exist:
`reproductions[].status` on the claims and `verification/<paper>/provenance.json` beside them.
The methods, statistics and citation layers are later still. Each reads warrant, does its own
work, and writes its own field; none of them edits warrant's value, and warrant never reads
theirs. That separation is the whole point of the reframe: one layer per question, each scored
against its own reading.

## Two halves of one layer

The layer has two halves, and they are not the same thing.

The **model's judgement** is the value written into the claim. A reader — a model, or a person —
is shown every claim with its sentence and its dossier, and returns a warrant level, a one-line
`why` that cites the dossier, and an `unsupported` flag. That reading is what `warrant:` holds.

The **rule** in `scripts/warrant.py` is the mechanical statement of what the tree's argument
implies. It is kept for scoring only, and it is never shown to the model — the prompt shows the
dossier and the vocabulary, and the rule's floor is revealed only *after* the model has judged.

A disagreement between the two is not an error to suppress. It is a signal for the adjudicator,
and it is the evidence for revising either the rule or the prompt: where they part company is
where the coarse rule and the reader's reading of the same argument diverge, and one of them
should move. And when a prompt or a model changes, agreement with the fixed rule measures the
drift the change introduced. The rule is the stable yardstick; the reading is what it measures.

## The layer

`warrant` is a feature layer, paper scope, with a model call: it assembles each claim's dossier
from the tree, shows a reader the dossier and the vocabulary, and writes `warrant:`,
`warrant_why:` and `warrant_from:` into each claim file, the last being the argument keys the
reading was made over. It needs `claim-tree`, `stance` and `prediction-outcome`, so it runs after
them and goes stale whenever any of them moves — the behaviour the format asked for by hand and
never got. It is approvable like any layer, and an adjudication verdict may override a value with
a reason; the override is a verdict, carried like the others, and the badge counts it.

## What happens to `epistemic`

While the draft is tried, `epistemic` is left untouched: the layer writes the three warrant keys
after it and does not remove it. Retiring `epistemic` from the format, the schema page and the
readers' output — and mapping `warrant` where `export_mira.py` mapped `epistemic` — is a later
step, taken once the reading has been read.

## How the rule is evaluated

The rule is a scheme, and it is scored the way the procedure note says a scheme is: against a
reading. The Gädeke adjudication produces the adjudicator's own view of what each claim's
argument rests on, and agreement between that and the rule's output is the first measurement. The
first run records the model's reading beside the rule's floor, overall and by role, with every
disagreement laid out — the model's `why` beside the rule's fired keys — so the adjudicator can
see where the coarse rule and the reader diverge and decide which should move.

## Rulings (2026-09-13)

The version-2 rule follows these rulings on the version-1 draft:

- Warrant reasons from the argument the tree records, not from the paper's confidence and not
  from any reproduction. The confidence clause and the reproduction clauses are removed.
- Checking — verification, a methods assessment, a statistics check, a citation check — is each a
  separate later layer that writes a modifier beside the warrant; `contested` from a failed
  reproduction is the verification layer's word, and verification declares first because its
  records exist.
- The dossier holds the argument only: role, stance, sentence; prediction outcomes; validating
  controls; rivals ruled out or standing; incoming supports/extends/confirms/refutes; what it
  requires and is part of, with roles; what it interprets. No reproductions, no verification, no
  confidence.
- The graded strong/moderate/weak rule reads confirmations, controls and support; an empty
  dossier is weak and unassessed. Interpretation is capped at moderate and steps below what it
  interprets; synthesis reads agreement. Propagation bounds within a role group only.
- The rule is kept for scoring only and never shown to the model; a model–rule disagreement is a
  signal for the adjudicator, and agreement measures drift when the prompt or model changes.

## Cost

The rule and the layer, with tests: one day. The first run on ten papers: minutes for the
mechanical floor; a reading per paper for the model half.

## What this does not decide

The thresholds, which the first reading will move. How, precisely, a checking layer's modifier
composes with the warrant it sits beside — whether it overrides, annotates, or both. Whether an
interpretation that interprets only a `contested` claim should read `weak` (as the rule now
floors it) or carry the taint further. Whether cross-paper support, when it exists, raises a
warrant or only counts. Whether `warrant` should also be shown as a number; the inputs are
recorded, so a number can be derived later without re-running.
