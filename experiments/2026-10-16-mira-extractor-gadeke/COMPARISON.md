# MIRA's extractor on Gädeke et al. (2026), against the hand-built MIRA-native graph

*Run of 2026-10-16. Inputs and outputs in this directory: `gadeke-2026-guilt-insula.md` (the paper, 89,276 characters, rendered from the eLife JATS via the corpus's article JSON), `prompt.md` (the extractor's exact prompt), `answer.json` (the raw reply of one Claude subagent, model `claude-fable-5-1`), and the replayed `gadeke-2026-guilt-insula.mira.jsonld` and `.graph.json`. The reference is `claim-graphs/docs/design/2026-10-07-gadeke-core.jsonld`, the appendix of the MIRA-native vocabulary note built by hand from our 68 claim files.*

## What was run

The extractor was used exactly as its skill prescribes, with the one model call answered by a Claude Code subagent rather than the pinned OpenRouter model. The prompt was dumped by the CLI, so it is character for character what the live call would send; the subagent read it in full and wrote only the JSON object; the CLI then parsed, folded, checked the grammar and projected it to JSON-LD in the ordinary way. The CLI dropped nothing: no malformed nodes, no dangling or ungrammatical edges, no flaked pieces. The JSON-LD validates against the vendored MIRA SHACL shapes (pin 483f0b2) with exit 0, conforming modulo the 317 instances of the three known generated-shape quirks that the validator downgrades to warnings (the `sh:in` lists on `sourceDocument`, `source` and `destination`, which the community's own sample data trips too). The paper text was fed as markdown rather than PDF, which sidesteps the pdf.js fragmentation the extractor is known to suffer from and is the most favourable condition for it. The difference from the earlier community run recorded in our vocabulary note, which fed the PDF to the pinned free model and got six Claims, is therefore two differences at once, the model and the input, and this run does not separate them. Six Claims against eighteen, and a graph of 95 nodes, is the scale of the gap.

The two graphs are nearly the same size in the layers that matter, Questions, Claims and Evidence, and differ mainly in what each spends its nodes on. The extractor spends them on Protocols, Requests and the cited literature; we spend them on the rivals the paper eliminates and on the hypothesis-to-prediction structure.

## Which of our 23 Claims the extractor recovers

Judged by meaning rather than wording, seventeen of our twenty-three Claims are present as Claims in the extractor's graph, but they are carried by only ten of its eighteen, because the extractor collapses what we keep apart. Our hypothesis H1 and its confirmed prediction P1a (happiness fell more after a partner's low outcome when the participant had chosen) are its c1, stated as a finding and supported by the interaction terms and the split-by-own-outcome tests from both studies. Our P1b (the Responsibility model fits best) is its c4. Our F-ownRPE is its c16, our F-risk its c6, our F-social-network its c12. Our F-agency and its interpretation I-responsibility-aversion merge into its c9, which states the main effect and the "weight of responsibility" reading in one sentence. Our I-simple-guilt is roughly its c8, "participants did experience guilt". On the imaging side, H2 and P2 (the insula carries the guilt effect) merge into c2; H2b, P2b and I-IFG (insula to right IFG coupling, and what it means) merge into c3; H3, P3 and I-STS (the STS tracks the partner's surprises when the participant caused them) merge into c5. This is the same collapse our vocabulary note itself anticipated: a confirmed prediction and the finding that confirmed it are one Claim in MIRA, and the extractor goes one step further and folds the hypothesis and the interpretation into that node too. Consequently our five `entails` edges, hypothesis to prediction, have no counterpart at all; there is nothing for them to join.

Two more of ours are present only as Evidence. F-rewards (happiness tracked both players' rewards) is its e9 and e10, which it attaches to a Claim we do not have, c14, "participants were not emotionally detached from the consequences of their actions on their partner, even though the partner's decisions were made by an algorithm". That is a defensible reading of what the authors use the partner-reward effect for in the Discussion, and arguably better placed than ours. F-signature (the insula response matched the published guilt signature) is its e35, supporting c8. One of ours, F-signature-individual (the match did not track individual guilt sensitivity), survives only as a clause inside e35's description, which means it has no identity in the graph and nothing can cite it. The remaining three of ours, R-agency, R-own and R-risk, are the rival explanations, and they are absent as Claims. I return to them below.

## What the extractor produces that we lack

Eight of its Claims have no counterpart among our Claims, and they fall into three kinds. Four are observations we hold as Evidence and it promotes to Claims: c15 (choice tracked the expected-value difference; our E-value-sensitive), c13 (ventral striatum for risky choice and expected reward; our E-striatum-risky and E-manipulation), c18 (the icebreaker built a bond; our E-icebreaker) and c17 (STS information is relayed to IFG; our E-PPI-STS, which we file as an uncorrected result supporting P2b and it elevates to a Claim addressing the paper's main question). Two are literature claims it builds from cited works: c11 (the anterior insula is associated with guilt) and c10 (people attach a cost to responsibility for others' outcomes), each grounded by Evidence from a named cited study with its own Protocol. We hold the same literature as three generic, unresolved SourceDocuments; the extractor resolved four of them to Koban 2013, Yu 2014, Yu 2020 and Edelson 2018 and gave each a Study, a Protocol and a specific Evidence record. That is a genuinely richer provenance spine than ours, and it is the one place where the extractor's output is plainly better. The remaining two are c14, discussed above, and c19, the authors' own caveat that participants may have felt empathy, failure or bad luck rather than guilt, which it models as a Claim that `opposes` c8. We have no node for that caveat.

Beyond the Claims, the extractor emits six Requests with ten `request_target` edges, drawn from the Discussion's future-work paragraph, each targeted at the Claims it bears on. Our hand-built graph has no Requests because our vocabulary has nothing to translate into them, and these six are well chosen. It also emits fifteen paper Protocols to our six, because its prompt's rule 3 asks for "the procedure as specified" per distinct method: the icebreaker, the happiness probe, the logistic regression, the risk premium, the CARA fit, the five happiness models, parameter recovery, the happiness LMM, acquisition and preprocessing, GLM1, the ROI mixed models, GLM2, the gPPI analysis and the signature comparison. Ours group these into task, happiness modelling, LMM, GLM2, ROI and PPI. The finer grain is more useful for reuse across papers and costs nothing in accuracy; each of its Protocol texts is a faithful compression of the Methods.

On Evidence the coverage is close to equal, with different splitting. The extractor pairs every behavioural result by study (e1/e2, e3/e4, e5/e6 and so on) where we mostly merge the two studies into one record and split only the guilt effect. It lacks five observations we keep as distinct records: the LMM model-selection result, the lottery-versus-safe localiser that defines the ROIs, the STS's tracking of the participant's own predictions in both conditions, the AIC result on which Responsibility Redux wins, and the individual-differences null on the signature. It merges our two insula ROI records into one (e29) and keeps the voxel-wise cluster separate (e30), which is sensible. Its Evidence texts carry the statistics inline, which the prompt's rule 4 asks for, and they are accurate against the paper.

## Anchors

The extractor's engine never checks anchors against the text, so I did, mechanically, by substring search over the markdown the subagent read. All 95 node anchors and all 13 edge anchors are present verbatim: 108 of 108, with no failures even before normalising curly quotes, en dashes and whitespace. Most are the 8 to 15 words the prompt asks for; a few run to 30 words where the model copied a whole statistical clause. This is a property of the answering model and the clean input rather than of the extractor, and it should not be expected to transfer to the pinned free model on a PDF. It does mean that for this run every record is locatable in the paper, which is the condition under which an extraction can be audited at all. The edge anchors are sparse, 13 of 139, because the prompt asks for an edge anchor only when a single passage states the relation, and the model applied that conservatively, anchoring mainly the `addresses` and claim-to-claim edges.

## The rival explanations

The paper eliminates three alternatives to the guilt reading of its behavioural effect, and our hand-built graph gives each a Claim node that Evidence `opposes`: that the extra drop in happiness is disappointment at the participant's own outcome (R-own, opposed by the split-by-own-outcome result), that choosing for someone else changes risk taking and that this explains the condition differences (R-risk, opposed by the risk-preference findings), and that the happiness cost of deciding is general agency aversion rather than guilt (R-agency, opposed by the interaction holding on top of the main effect). MIRA has no vocabulary for a rival except a Claim the authors do not hold, opposed by Evidence; our note argued that this is nonetheless expressible, and the hand-built graph shows it is.

The extractor does not produce any of the three, and the way it fails is instructive, because it fails three different ways. The own-outcome rival disappears into the texture of the graph: the control result that eliminates it is present and correct, in e22 and e23 ("both when the participant received the high outcome and the low outcome"), and c1's description notes that the effect "occurs whether the participant received the high or low lottery outcome", but there is no node for the explanation that result rules out, so there is no `opposes` edge and nothing to tell a reader that an alternative was tested. The risk rival is present with its polarity inverted: the extractor states the authors' conclusion c6, "there is no strong evidence that risk preferences differ", and then lets the two Study 1 results that do show a difference `oppose` it, while the four null results `support` it. That is an honest representation of the evidence, and it is the only place in the graph where Evidence opposes anything, but it says nothing about why the authors cared about risk preferences, namely as a confound for the guilt effect. The agency rival is turned into a positive finding: c9 states that being the decider lowers happiness, e18 and e19 support it, and c10 (responsibility aversion, from Edelson 2018) supports c9 in turn. Nothing in the graph says that this main effect does not explain the guilt interaction, which is what the paper's argument requires and what our P1a `opposes` R-agency edge encodes.

The reason is in the prompt rather than in the schema. The prompt defines a Claim as "an atomic, generalized assertion the authors make", instructs the model to prefer a smaller graph, and to extract a Claim only when it "does work" in the graph. A rival explanation is precisely not an assertion the authors make, and a model following the definition literally will not create one, even though rule 7 permits a Claim to oppose a Claim and the `opposes` relation is in the grammar. The three `opposes` edges it did emit confirm the pattern: two make Evidence oppose the authors' own claim, and one makes the authors' own caveat (c19) oppose their own conclusion (c8). None makes Evidence oppose a rival the authors eliminated. So the structure MIRA can express, and our graph does express, is not one this prompt will elicit. If the community wants eliminated alternatives in the graph, the prompt needs a clause saying that a hypothesis the authors test and reject is a Claim, that the result rejecting it `opposes` that Claim, and that this is work enough to justify the node.

## What to take from this

Fed a clean text and answered by a strong model, MIRA's extractor produces a graph of the same scale as ours that recovers seventeen of our twenty-three Claims, every anchor verbatim, and adds things we lack: resolved cited works with their own provenance spine, six Requests, and Protocols at a grain fit for reuse. What it loses is argumentative structure. Hypotheses, predictions and interpretations collapse into findings, so there is no `entails` layer and no way to see which findings the authors predicted; and the rivals the paper eliminated are absent, so a reader cannot see what the controls were for. Both losses are consequences of a prompt that defines a Claim as what the authors assert, not of the schema, and the second is fixable with one rule. The first is the design question our vocabulary note raised and this run does not settle: whether a hypothesis-to-prediction relation is worth adding to MIRA, or whether the collapse is acceptable because the finding-level Claim is what most readers want.
