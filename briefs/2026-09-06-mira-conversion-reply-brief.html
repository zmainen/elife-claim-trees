The ask

Paul Shannon, 27 August — "Progress on building claim extraction into our article software"

eLife has implemented OXA in Reviewed Preprints and is now integrating extracted claims into the article reading experience itself. They want MIRA as the input format, and they want the Gädeke 2026 claims converted as the pilot.

Paul notes that eLife could do the conversion themselves — but that we are better placed to confirm it is correct. So the deliverable is not the file. It is the evidence that the mapping is faithful, and that the claims themselves hold.

One name, not two

Paul writes "a MIRA graph or Discourse Graph format." These are the same thing. Discourse Graph is Joel Chan's conceptual framework; MIRA is its formal, inter-operable specification, maintained by Marc-Antoine Parent and running at 10+ life-science labs. There is no choice to make — send MIRA JSON-LD. OXA is a different layer: it structures the document, MIRA structures the argument. Our ClaimGraph RFC is the proposed bridge, and it is ours, not yet upstream.

What changed today

This claim had been sitting at WARN since March, logged as "nibabel not installed." That message was wrong twice over. The library was fine; the script could not find the per-participant map because the deposit ships it as .nii.zip and the search pattern only matched .nii. Unzipped, it then failed for a second reason: the mask was being resampled onto the four-dimensional image rather than its three-dimensional spatial grid, which produces a malformed transform. Resampling to shape[:3] fixes it, and the claim passes.

Both are one-line fixes in verify.py, and neither is applied yet — the run above used a scratch copy, so your repository is untouched.

What would make the chain airtight

Four gaps between "we ran it" and "anyone can audit it"

The chain holds, but parts of it are held together by search patterns rather than by fixed references. If this is going to a publisher as a reproducibility demonstration, these are worth closing first.

  • Two claims find their data by pattern-matching, not by name. The happiness and guilt checks glob the CSV directory and pick a file whose columns look right. It works, but it means the chain cannot be audited by reading it — a reader cannot tell which of the forty CSVs was used. Pin the exact filenames.
  • The deposit is not pinned to a revision. The script clones whatever is at the tip of the authors' repository today. Record the commit hash so the numbers stay attached to a specific state of the data.
  • The reproduced values do not live in the claim files. Each claim's reproductions block records a status and a note, but not the function that produced it or the file it read. Writing the chain back into the claims is what makes it machine-readable — and it is precisely what MIRA has no room for, which makes it the sharpest example for the extension proposal.
  • The recorded Yu–Koban numbers disagree with today's run. The claim file says sign p = 0.017 and Wilcoxon p = 0.042; today's run gives 0.0083 and 0.0211. Same direction, same conclusion, different figures — presumably a different resampling choice in an earlier attempt. Whichever is right, the file should carry the number the current code actually produces.

What MIRA drops

46 of the 75 relations in the Gädeke graph — 61% — have no MIRA equivalent. Measured by the exporter, not estimated.

The conversion works and is lossy, in ways better named than discovered. Three losses are structural.

What we haveIn MIRAConsequence for a reader
entails, derived-from (10)
hypothesis → prediction		 No predicate The deductive spine vanishes. Three hypotheses and four predictions flatten into one undifferentiated set — you cannot tell which prediction belongs to which hypothesis.
tests vs confirms
also validates, extends		 All → mira:supports A result designed in advance to test a prediction becomes indistinguishable from one that merely agrees after the fact.
reproductions
the entire chain above		 No node type Every verification in this brief is dropped on export. There is nowhere in MIRA to say that a claim was checked, by what code, against what data.
scopes (29), interprets (5), enables-method (2) No predicate The two scope claims each constrain 12+ downstream claims. Those constraints silently detach.
— mira:Question required MIRA wants an explicit question node per claim. We have none; they must be synthesised from hypothesis text, which is an editorial act, not a conversion.
The sharpest example

partner-algorithm-deception-assumption records that participants believed they were harming a person when the partner was in fact an expected-value-maximising algorithm — a validity constraint inherited by every social claim in the paper. In our format it attaches to all twelve. In MIRA it becomes a free-floating claim attached to nothing. That is not a conversion oversight; it is a gap in the standard.

One gap that is ours, not MIRA's

Only one of the 27 claims is prior work — the Yu & Koban guilt signature. Partly that is the paper's nature, being focused and empirical rather than a review; mostly it is because the pipeline extracted the paper's own argument and never made a second pass over its reference list. Better to name this to eLife than let them find it.

The proposal

Three additions to MIRA, ranked by what each buys a reader

The framing that works is not "MIRA is missing things," but: here are three additions that would let the claims layer drive the article experience rather than merely annotate it.

1 · A verification record type

MIRA has no concept of anyone having checked anything. A node carrying who checked, when, against which data, with what code, and what came back is the most original thing we have — and the chain above is the working demonstration, not a sketch. For a publisher whose reputation rests on reproducibility this is the strongest of the three. Reader payoff: a trust signal on the claim itself, traceable to code.

2 · Derivation predicates

Two predicates, entails (hypothesis → prediction) and tests (evidence → prediction, directional), recover the argument's logical backbone. Reader payoff: you can walk the paper from question to data.

3 · Edge precision

Cheapest of the three: rather than collapsing tests, confirms, validates and extends into one relation, carry the distinction as a sub-property or qualifier on supports. Reader payoff: how much weight a piece of support deserves becomes legible.

Leave scopes, interprets, enables-method and requires out of the first proposal — they matter structurally but are harder to motivate to a platform team, and four asks dilute three good ones.

What I would change on the site

Seven changes to zmainen.github.io/elife-claim-trees, in priority order

The site is already a working prototype of the reading experience, which is why it is worth sending Paul. But its most distinctive asset — the chain from claim to code to data — is the thing it currently shows least. These are ordered by how much each strengthens the case to eLife.

1 · Make verification the front door, not a badge

A green tick is an assertion; the chain is evidence. Every verified claim should show, inline: the function that checks it, the file in the deposit it reads, the paper's value, the reproduced value, and a copy-paste command to run it. The three-minute reproducibility is the headline — put the command on the page and let a reader run it while they read.

2 · Publish the run log, including what failed

A dated table per paper, straight from verify.log — pass, warn and fail alike. A site that says 6 of 7, and here is exactly why the seventh did not run is far more credible to a publisher than one showing unbroken green. Today's history is a good example: a claim sat at WARN for five months behind a misleading error message.

3 · Navigate by argument, not by list

ClaimDAG already exists. Promote it from a view to the primary way in: enter at a hypothesis, walk down through its predictions to the results that test them. This is precisely the structure MIRA cannot carry, so making it the site's main interaction is also the strongest possible argument for the extension.

4 · Show scope inheritance where it bites

Gädeke's partner was a simulated algorithm, not a person — a constraint inherited by twelve downstream claims. Any claim carrying an inherited scope should say so on the claim page itself, as a persistent banner rather than a footnote. No other claims interface does this, and it is a genuinely better way to read a paper.

5 · Offer the formats as a download

A control on each paper page exporting HaaK YAML, OXA JSON, or MIRA JSON-LD. This turns the interop story from a claim into a button — and it means Paul can fetch the file himself instead of waiting for an attachment.

6 · Fold the comparison artifacts into standards.astro

Five one-off pages currently live outside the site, including the graph with the HaaK/MIRA toggle where you can watch the typed relations collapse. That toggle is the proposal in one click and belongs in the product, not in a scratch artifact.

7 · Publish the denominator

Twenty-seven claims, five with verification records, one prior-work node. Showing coverage honestly — including a plain "what we have not checked and why" panel, naming the claims that need MATLAB — is more persuasive than showing only the wins, and it invites exactly the collaboration you want.

You were right that the conversion is mechanical and the checking is the useful part. Two things came out of it.

First, the claims themselves verify. Seven of the paper's claims are now checked end to end against the authors' own deposit — the insula peak reproduces exactly at MNI [−28, 24, −4]; the happiness effects come back at β = 0.34 and 0.40 against 0.33 and 0.39 reported; the R² values land within 0.002; and the Yu & Koban signature replication holds at sign-test p = 0.008 across 40 participants. That last point may be the more useful finding for you: a substantial fraction of a paper's claims can be independently checked from a good deposit alone.

Second, the conversion is lossy in ways I think matter for what you are building. Forty-six of the seventy-five typed relations in the Gädeke graph — 61% — have no MIRA equivalent. Across all fourteen papers we have extracted, the figure is 475 of 789, so this is not a quirk of one paper:

  • The hypothesis → prediction → result chain disappears — MIRA has no predicate for entailment, so the three hypotheses and their predictions flatten into one undifferentiated set and a reader cannot follow the argument.
  • A result designed to test a specific prediction is indistinguishable from one that merely agrees with it; both become supports.
  • Scope constraints detach. Gädeke's partner was a simulated expected-value-maximising algorithm rather than a real person — a constraint that in our format attaches to all twelve social claims that inherit it, and in MIRA attaches to nothing.
  • And the verification above has nowhere to live. MIRA has no node type for "this was checked, here is the code, here is the data, here is what came back."

None of that is a conversion problem we can fix at our end — they are gaps in the schema, and the last one especially seems worth solving rather than working around.
