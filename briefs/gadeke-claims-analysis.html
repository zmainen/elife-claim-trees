The paper

Contributions of insula and superior temporal sulcus to interpersonal guilt and responsibility in social decisions

Maria Gädeke, Tom Eric Willems, Omar Salah Ahmed, Bernd Weber, Rene Hurlemann, Johannes Schultz

eLife 14 · Version of Record, 24 March 2026 10.7554/eLife.105391 Authors' data deposit OpenNeuro ds005588

The shape of the argument

27 claims across seven roles

3 4 11 5 3
3 hypotheses 4 predictions 11 empirical 5 controls 1 interpretation 2 scope · 1 prior work

The paper argues three things: that the anterior insula encodes responsibility-contingent interpersonal guilt; that momentary happiness weights partner reward-prediction errors by the participant's own responsibility for them; and that the superior temporal sulcus represents the partner's affective state specifically under that responsibility. Each hypothesis entails one or two predictions, and each prediction is tested by named empirical claims. Five controls establish that the paradigm behaves as intended, and two scope claims constrain everything downstream.

The five controls are worth noting as a marker of extraction quality: they are not findings, they are the reasons to believe the findings, and a claim graph that omits them is flattering rather than faithful.

The important finding

These checks do not touch the raw imaging data. They run against the authors' own deposit of analysis tables and thresholded statistical maps — about 50 MB, cloned by the script, roughly three minutes end to end on a laptop. No imaging pipeline, no MATLAB, no SPM12.

Re-analysing from raw data is a different and much larger job: OpenNeuro ds005588, about 15 GB, three hours, and first-level GLMs for every subject. It was not needed for anything above. The generalisable point for a publisher is that a well-constructed deposit makes a substantial fraction of a paper's claims checkable by anyone, cheaply.

The Yu–Koban check, and why it sat broken for five months

This claim had been recorded as a partial result since March while the committed script could not actually run it, logging "nibabel not installed." That message was wrong on both counts. The library was present. The script could not find the per-participant contrast map because the deposit ships it as guiltEffectEachPartic.nii.zip and the file-search pattern only matched .nii. Unzipped, it failed for a second, separate reason: the signature mask was being resampled onto the four-dimensional image rather than onto its three-dimensional spatial grid, which produces a malformed transform.

With both corrected, the check runs: the Yu & Koban guilt signature mask covers 50,860 voxels, and the per-participant spatial dot products are positive in 28 of 40 participants — sign test p = 0.0083, Wilcoxon p = 0.0211. The paper's claim holds.

The March record gives p = 0.017 and p = 0.042 for the same test. Same direction, same conclusion, different figures, presumably a different resampling choice in an implementation that has since been lost. That discrepancy is unresolved, and both numbers are now recorded in the claim file rather than one quietly replacing the other.

What has not been checked, and why

Twenty of the 27 claims carry no verification record. This is not uniform neglect — the reasons differ and are worth separating:

  • Hypotheses and predictions (7 claims) are not the kind of thing that reproduces. They are the paper's argument, not its results. Nothing is missing here.
  • The computational-model claims need the authors' MATLAB fitting code. The deposit includes fitted parameters but not a runnable path from data to fit.
  • The whole-brain GLM claims need SPM12 and a full re-run — precomputed maps are deposited, but checking them means redoing the analysis.
  • Several behavioural claims are simply not yet written up as checks, and could be. This is the most tractable remaining work.

The lesson worth keeping

Every one of these errors came from reading the claim files and reasoning about them, rather than running the code they point to. The records described the verification accurately enough to be quoted and badly enough to be misread — and a reader working carefully from them still concluded the opposite of the truth. That is an argument for the chain being machine-checkable rather than narrated: a record that names its function, its input file and its output value cannot drift from what the code does without the drift being visible.
